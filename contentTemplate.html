Ocean Health & Biodiversity

Our oceans cover over 70% of the Earth's surface and sustain all life on our planet. SDG 14 — Life Below Water — calls on all of us to protect and restore marine ecosystems before it is too late. This page explores why oceans matter, the threats they face, and the concrete steps you can take today.

Introduction

The ocean is Earth's life-support system. It regulates climate, produces more than half of the oxygen we breathe, absorbs roughly 30% of all CO₂ emissions, and provides food and livelihoods for more than three billion people worldwide.

Yet despite its fundamental importance, the ocean is under unprecedented stress. Pollution, overfishing, habitat destruction, and climate-driven warming are pushing marine systems toward tipping points that could prove irreversible within our lifetimes.

30% of CO₂ absorbed by oceans annually
50% of Earth's oxygen produced by marine life
3B+ people depend on oceans for food & income

Why It Matters to Students

You might be studying far from the coast, but the ocean shapes your world in ways you interact with every day — from the seafood in your canteen to the weather outside your window and the cotton in your clothing (produced with water that cycles through ocean evaporation).

💡 Did you know? Ocean acidification — caused by excess CO₂ — has already increased ocean acidity by 26% since the Industrial Revolution, threatening shellfish, coral, and the entire food chains that depend on them.

Economic Relevance

The global ocean economy is valued at over $1.5 trillion annually. Tourism, fisheries, shipping, and emerging blue-economy industries all depend on healthy seas. As future professionals, decisions you make in engineering, business, law, design, or science will intersect with ocean health.

Social Justice Angle

Coastal and island communities — often among the world's most economically marginalised populations — bear the greatest cost of ocean degradation. Declining fish stocks mean hunger and poverty. Rising seas mean displacement. Protecting the ocean is inseparable from protecting human rights.

Key Threats Facing Our Oceans

Plastic Pollution

Around 8 million tonnes of plastic enter the ocean every year — equivalent to dumping a rubbish truck's worth every single minute. Microplastics have now been found in the deepest ocean trenches and in human blood. Marine animals mistake plastic for food, causing starvation, internal injury, and death.

Overfishing

The UN Food and Agriculture Organisation estimates that over a third of global fish stocks are now harvested at biologically unsustainable levels. Without recovery time, entire species and the ecosystems they anchor risk collapse.

Ocean Warming & Acidification

Oceans have absorbed over 90% of the excess heat generated by climate change. Warmer, more acidic water bleaches coral reefs, disrupts breeding cycles, and forces species to migrate — or face extinction.

  • 50% of the world's coral reefs have been lost since the 1950s
  • Ocean temperatures have risen by an average of 0.13°C per decade
  • Dead zones — low-oxygen areas hostile to marine life — have quadrupled since 1950
  • Seagrass meadows, critical nurseries for fish, are declining at 7% per year

Actions You Can Take as a Student

You don't need to be a marine biologist or an environmental activist to make a difference. Here are practical, realistic actions that every student can take right now:

In Your Daily Life

  1. Refuse single-use plastics — carry a reusable bottle, bag, and coffee cup.
  2. Choose sustainable seafood — use apps like "Good Fish Guide" when shopping or eating out.
  3. Reduce your carbon footprint — ocean warming is driven by CO₂; cutting your emissions helps.
  4. Never pour chemicals or medication down the drain — they end up in waterways and the sea.

As Part of a Community

  1. Join or organise a beach or river clean-up — local waterways connect directly to the ocean.
  2. Advocate at your university — push for plastic-free policies in your canteen and campus shops.
  3. Share knowledge — social media reach can spread ocean-positive awareness further than any campaign.

🌊 Even landlocked students matter: every river, stream, and drain eventually leads to the sea. Your local environment is connected to global ocean health.

Success Stories

Progress is possible. Around the world, communities, governments, and young activists have shown that ocean protection works when people take action.

Marine Protected Areas

Where MPAs have been established and enforced, fish populations have recovered significantly — sometimes doubling within a decade. The UK's network of MPAs now covers around 38% of its waters.

Seagrass Restoration in Wales

Project Seagrass — a charity partly founded by researchers at Cardiff University — has successfully restored seagrass meadows in Dale Bay, Wales. Seagrass stores carbon 35 times faster than rainforests and is a critical nursery for juvenile fish.

The Great Barrier Reef Recovery Efforts

Australian scientists and the government have invested in coral reef restoration programmes including coral aquaculture and heat-resistant coral breeding. Early results in some reef sections show genuine recovery when local stressors are reduced alongside climate action.

Get Involved

Ready to turn awareness into action? Here are some places to start:

  • Surfers Against Sewage — UK-based ocean charity running beach cleans and campaigns.
  • Project Seagrass — volunteer and citizen science opportunities.
  • Oceana — global advocacy organisation with student programmes.
  • Marine Conservation Society — good fish guides, volunteer events, policy campaigns.
